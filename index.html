I

Why this exists

The answer that sounds right is the dangerous one

My campaign notes were spread across six documents, two of which contradicted the other four. Players asked me the same questions between sessions — who was that merchant, what did we actually agree to, whose brother is the king — and I answered from memory, badly, and sometimes differently than last time.

The obvious fix is to ask an AI, and it fails in a specific way: a general chatbot handed your setting answers every question confidently, including the ones your documents say nothing about.

So the whole design is one idea: it may only tell you what your documents say, and it must show you where it got it. Answer a logged gap once and that answer is reused from then on.

Nobody needs this until the evening a player asks something specific and four people remember it four different ways. Then it is the only thing you want.

II

When it doesn't know

The best thing it does is admit defeat

Any assistant can answer a question your documents cover. The one that decides whether you can trust it at the table is the question they don't — and the useful behaviour there is to stop, say so, and remember that you were asked.

A ruling recorded this way is cited like any other source, so six months later nobody has to remember whether it was decided or imagined. What people actually ask it →

III

How it compares

Why not use something that already exists?

Often you should. Most people arrive here having already pasted their notes into ChatGPT — good software, and for a question your documents genuinely answer it will usually answer correctly. The trouble is the question they don't.

NotebookLM is the closest comparison, and on its own ground it is better than this. Google's budget, excellent retrieval, exact citations, nothing to install. If you are working alone through a pile of documents, use it. The difference is not quality — it is what the two things are built for.

The second column is also this project. The Foundry module answers on its own, out of the journal entries in the folders you choose, with nothing else installed — that is Lite, and for a campaign that already lives in Foundry it may be the whole answer. It sits in the grid because the honest way to show what the full application adds is to put the two beside each other and let the rows say it.

IV

How it works

Four stages, and one of them is the point

A question goes in one end. Stage two is the one you cannot configure away, and the reason the answer at the far end can be trusted.

  1. 1

    Find what bears on the question

    Reads a folder on your disk — PDF, DOCX, Markdown, plain text — or an Obsidian vault, read-only, nothing moved. Passage-level retrieval then picks out the parts that matter, so a large campaign is not a large bill.

  2. 2

    Force the rules on top

    Cite every claim; refuse rather than invent. The block is force-appended to every prompt, so editing the system prompt — or picking a persona — cannot remove it.

    The reason this exists

  3. 3

    Ask your model

    Gemini, ~400 models through OpenRouter, a Claude subscription, or one running on your own machine. Swappable mid-campaign.

  4. 4

    Check what came back

    A refusal is labelled rather than served as content in the archivist's voice — and the question it could not answer lands in your dashboard as a lore gap.

The corpus travels with every question, so every cloud request pins a zero-data-retention routing floor. How the retrieval pipeline works →

V

Ask it anything

Not every question is a lookup

Most of what gets asked at a table is not "when did Dunmar fall". It is an argument two players are having, or a joke someone wants settled with evidence. Those work — and they work for the same reason the serious ones do. It can only answer from what you wrote down, and it shows you where.

And it doesn't have to sound like a librarian

A persona replaces the whole voice, and the citation rules survive it. Same question, same corpus, same sources — two of the eight that ship:

And you decide whether it's allowed to guess

"Who would lose a duel to a chicken" is not in anybody's notes, so by default it is refused like any other question the documents don't answer. Turn on Speculative Mode and it will reason from what the chronicle establishes about each character — then cite the evidence it used and tag the conclusion, so a guess can never be mistaken later for something you wrote down.

Mid-session, in the chat bar

Type /tusk and the answer posts into the chat log without anyone leaving the game. A per-world access mode decides whether players may ask at all, and whether the answer is whispered to them or posted for the table — because not every answer is one the party should have.

Wants a second, separate install — the Tusk’s Vault module, searchable by name in Foundry’s own Install Module browser, or from its repository. It runs two ways, chosen in Module Settings.

Lite needs nothing else at all: it answers from the journal entries in one Foundry folder, citing the entry it quoted and honouring your journal permissions, so a GM-only note stays GM-only. That is a working archivist in about two minutes, without downloading Vault.

Bridge is where this page comes in. Point the module at Vault running on the GM’s machine and the answers stop being limited to one journal folder — they come from everything you keep, Word and PDF and Markdown and a whole Obsidian vault, with your model of choice and your API key never in a browser. Lite is the trial; Bridge is the archive.

VIII

What leaves your machine

You're handing this your whole campaign

Years of somebody's worldbuilding, and often things said at a table that were never meant to leave it. So it's worth being exact about where everything goes. Almost all of it stays on the machine you installed it on.

  • No account, no sign-up, no licence check.
  • No telemetry, no analytics, nothing phoning home. Ever.
  • Keys encrypted at rest and tied to the machine — copying the file elsewhere gains nothing.
  • Cloud requests pin a zero-data-retention floor, because the corpus travels with the question.
  • MIT licensed, every line readable, including the setup scripts.

One person, one Windows PC, no support desk — which is why the feedback button sits next to the coffee one in the app. Telling me what broke gets it seen; it does not promise the fix sticks. The unvarnished version is here.

So what happens if I stop?

Worth asking of anything you hand years of worldbuilding. The answer splits cleanly.

And who should be allowed to ask?

A different question from where the data goes, and the one more likely to matter at a table. Anyone who can ask reaches the whole corpus, because retrieval follows the question — ask about a mystery and you get the notes about that mystery. That is what makes it useful, and it is worth knowing before you point it at a group.

Players will also try talking it into simply reciting what it was handed. Some of them will manage it. What comes out is your own lore — not your keys, your files, or anything else on the machine. The archivist has no filesystem, no shell and no network of its own, and that is tested rather than hoped for. So the thing to plan around is spoilers and timing, not a breach.

Which makes this a tool for a private Discord or a Foundry table of trusted players rather than a public server. Player questions are off by default on Foundry; on Discord the bot only reads the channels you put it in. What a prompt injection can and cannot do spells out both halves.

IX

Questions

The ones people actually ask

Does it make up lore when it does not know?
No — that is the whole point of the design. A canonical rule block is appended to every prompt at assembly time, so citation discipline is structural rather than configurable. When the answer is not in your documents the bot emits a fixed refusal phrase, and the question is logged in the dashboard's Lore Gaps tab so you can answer it once and have that answer reused afterwards.
Do my campaign documents get uploaded anywhere?
Your documents stay in a folder on your machine. Only the question and the snippets that answer it are sent to whichever model you chose, and if you run a local model through Ollama then nothing leaves the machine at all.
What does it cost to run?
The software is MIT-licensed and free forever. The only cost is the model you pick: Google Gemini's free tier covers most home games, OpenRouter is pay-as-you-go at roughly $0.60 to $4 per three-hour session, a Claude Pro or Max subscription costs nothing extra, and a local model through Ollama costs nothing at all.
Do I need to be technical to run it?
You need to be comfortable installing Node.js and running a script. On Windows there is a one-file installer that prints its whole plan and asks before it changes anything; on macOS and Linux it is a clone and one command.
Can players use it, or only the GM?
Both, and how much players can see is yours to set. In Foundry VTT there is a per-world access mode controlling whether players may ask at all, and answers can be whispered rather than posted publicly.
What file formats can it read?
PDF, DOCX, Markdown, plain text, JSON, YAML, CSV, HTML and RTF. It can also read an existing Obsidian vault directly, strictly read-only, without you having to move or copy anything.
Does it work with Foundry VTT?
Yes, through a companion module that pairs with your running Vault in one click. You ask with /tusk in the chat bar and the answer posts into the chat log, whispered or public depending on the world's access mode.
How is this different from asking ChatGPT about my campaign?
A general chatbot will answer every question confidently, including the ones your documents say nothing about, and the invented answer reads exactly like a real one. Vault cites the document behind every claim and refuses when it has no source, which is the behaviour that makes it usable at the table.
Does it only work for tabletop campaigns?
That is what it was built and tuned for — the prompts, citation styles and refusal wording all assume a game master and a table. But nothing in the machinery knows what a campaign is. It indexes a folder of documents, answers with citations, and refuses when the folder does not cover the question, so pointing it at a handbook, a wiki export, a set of house rules or any other body of documents gives you the same behaviour over those instead.
X

Start

Two things, then one command

You need Node 20 or newer and Git. That is the whole requirement — no Python, no Docker, no database, no admin rights, and no paid key: a free Gemini tier covers most home games, and a local model costs nothing at all.

One machine is supported so far

Tusk’s Vault is built and used daily on 64-bit Windows 10 / 11, and that is the only platform where a clean install has been run start to finish. Windows on ARM, macOS and Linux should work but are unverified — nothing in the code is Windows-only and the test suite runs on Linux on every commit, but nobody has yet booted the app on a Mac, a Linux desktop, or an ARM laptop.

Nothing here can harm your computer; the realistic failure is that the server does not start. If you try one of those, please say what happened — that is exactly what moves a platform to supported. The full platform table →
