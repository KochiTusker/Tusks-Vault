I

Why this exists

The answer that sounds right is the dangerous one

My campaign notes were spread across six documents, two of which contradicted the other four. Players asked me the same questions between sessions — who was that merchant, what did we actually agree to, whose brother is the king — and I answered from memory, badly, and sometimes differently than last time.

The obvious fix is to ask an AI, and it fails in a specific way: a general chatbot handed your setting answers every question confidently, including the ones your documents say nothing about.

So the whole design is one idea: it may only tell you what your documents say, and it must show you where it got it. Answer a logged gap once and that answer is reused from then on.

II

When it doesn't know

The best thing it does is admit defeat

Any assistant can answer a question your documents cover. The one that decides whether you can trust it at the table is the question they don't — and the useful behaviour there is to stop, say so, and remember that you were asked.

A ruling recorded this way is cited like any other source, so six months later nobody has to remember whether it was decided or imagined. What people actually ask it →

III

How it compares

Why not use something that already exists?

Often you should. Most people arrive here having already pasted their notes into ChatGPT — good software, and for a question your documents genuinely answer it will usually answer correctly. The trouble is the question they don't.

NotebookLM is the closest comparison, and on its own ground it is better than this. Google's budget, excellent retrieval, exact citations, nothing to install. If you are working alone through a pile of documents, use it. The difference is not quality — it is what the two things are built for.

IV

How it works

Four stages, and one of them is the point

A question goes in one end. Stage two is the one you cannot configure away, and the reason the answer at the far end can be trusted.

  1. 1

    Find what bears on the question

    Reads a folder on your disk — PDF, DOCX, Markdown, plain text — or an Obsidian vault, read-only, nothing moved. Passage-level retrieval then picks out the parts that matter, so a large campaign is not a large bill.

  2. 2

    Force the rules on top

    Cite every claim; refuse rather than invent. The block is force-appended to every prompt, so editing the system prompt — or picking a persona — cannot remove it.

    The reason this exists

  3. 3

    Ask your model

    Gemini, ~400 models through OpenRouter, a Claude subscription, or one running on your own machine. Swappable mid-campaign.

  4. 4

    Check what came back

    A refusal is labelled rather than served as content in the archivist's voice — and the question it could not answer lands in your dashboard as a lore gap.

The corpus travels with every question, so every cloud request pins a zero-data-retention routing floor. How the retrieval pipeline works →

V

Ask it anything

Not every question is a lookup

Most of what gets asked at a table is not "when did Dunmar fall". It is an argument two players are having, or a joke someone wants settled with evidence. Those work — and they work for the same reason the serious ones do. It can only answer from what you wrote down, and it shows you where.

And it doesn't have to sound like a librarian

A persona replaces the whole voice, and the citation rules survive it. Same question, same corpus, same sources — two of the eight that ship:

And you decide whether it's allowed to guess

"Who would lose a duel to a chicken" is not in anybody's notes, so by default it is refused like any other question the documents don't answer. Turn on Speculative Mode and it will reason from what the chronicle establishes about each character — then cite the evidence it used and tag the conclusion, so a guess can never be mistaken later for something you wrote down.

VIII

What leaves your machine

You're handing this your whole campaign

Years of somebody's worldbuilding, and often things said at a table that were never meant to leave it. So it's worth being exact about where everything goes. Almost all of it stays on the machine you installed it on.

  • No account, no sign-up, no licence check.
  • No telemetry, no analytics, nothing phoning home. Ever.
  • Keys encrypted at rest and tied to the machine — copying the file elsewhere gains nothing.
  • Cloud requests pin a zero-data-retention floor, because the corpus travels with the question.
  • MIT licensed, every line readable, including the setup scripts.

One person, one Windows PC, no support desk — which is why the feedback button sits next to the coffee one in the app. Telling me what broke gets it seen; it does not promise the fix sticks. The unvarnished version is here.

X

Start

Two things, then one command

You need Node 20 or newer and Git. That is the whole requirement — no Python, no Docker, no database, no admin rights, and no paid key: a free Gemini tier covers most home games, and a local model costs nothing at all.

One machine is supported so far

Tusk’s Vault is built and used daily on 64-bit Windows 10 / 11, and that is the only platform where a clean install has been run start to finish. Windows on ARM, macOS and Linux should work but are unverified — nothing in the code is Windows-only and the test suite runs on Linux on every commit, but nobody has yet booted the app on a Mac, a Linux desktop, or an ARM laptop.

Nothing here can harm your computer; the realistic failure is that the server does not start. If you try one of those, please say what happened — that is exactly what moves a platform to supported. The full platform table →
